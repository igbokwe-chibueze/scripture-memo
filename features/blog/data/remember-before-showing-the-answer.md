When a word is missing from a verse, showing the answer immediately would be convenient. You could read it, recognize it, and move on. Why does Scripture Memo instead ask you to try remembering?

Because the attempt is part of the learning activity. We want to give you opportunities to bring the wording to mind, not only opportunities to see it again.

This approach is called retrieval practice: trying to recover information from memory. In a memorization game, a hidden word creates a small occasion to practice that recovery.

## Reading and remembering do different jobs

Reading gives you access to the passage. It lets you encounter the wording, consider the context, and check what is correct. Trying to supply a missing word asks you to work with what you can remember from that encounter.

Both belong in the journey. We would not ask someone to recall a passage they had never had an opportunity to study. We also would not want the passage to remain so fully supplied that the learner never has to produce any of it.

Think of the difference between reading a familiar recipe and preparing its next step without looking. The second activity can reveal what you actually need the recipe to tell you. It also gives you a reason to attend carefully when you check it again.

## What retrieval research contributes

Karpicke and Roediger studied foreign-language vocabulary learning and found that repeated retrieval after initial learning supported later recall. Under their experimental conditions, additional study did not produce the same benefit. The study helps explain why attempting to remember is treated as a learning activity rather than merely a final test. [Read the retrieval study.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18276894/)

A broader review by Dunlosky and colleagues rated practice testing and distributed practice highly for their usefulness across learning conditions. [Read the review of learning techniques.](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/journals/pspi/learning-techniques.html)

Those findings support the general principle. They do not establish that every Scripture Memo exercise, or our exact sequence of exercises, produces a particular improvement in Scripture recall. The connection to our product is a design application that we should continue evaluating.

## What an honest attempt looks like

An honest attempt does not require a long struggle. Pause and consider the surrounding phrase. Ask yourself which word belongs there. Try to bring it to mind before requesting extra help.

Sometimes the answer will arrive quickly. Sometimes you will remember only part of it. Sometimes you will discover that you do not know it yet. Each outcome gives you information about your next step.

If help is available and needed, use it. Examine the correct word, connect it to the sentence, and try again. The intention is to leave room for recall, not to make assistance feel shameful.

Our Hint System remains separate from Cue, the fourth game mode. Cue supplies first-letter support as part of its exercise. Hints provide additional assistance where the Journey Stage allows it. Learn and Recall permit hints; Strengthen and Master do not.

## How the game gives you progressively less support

In Drag & Drop, you choose from a word bank. That can help you recognize the wording and attend to where it belongs. Puzzle works with larger word groups, while Swap asks you to restore the correct order.

Cue then asks you to supply missing words with their initial letters as support. Fill removes that particular support. Across Glimmer, Glow, and Radiance, larger portions of the passage are hidden as well.

Our intention is to provide a path from supported reconstruction toward more independent production. We do not treat dragging a visible answer and typing an absent answer as exactly the same kind of retrieval.

For the learner, this means a mode can feel harder even though the passage is unchanged. The screen may now be giving you less of what you previously relied on. That is a reason to examine the task, not automatically a reason to conclude that you are making no progress.

## What to do when you remember incorrectly

Suppose you enter a word that makes sense but is not the wording of your selected Bible translation. Stop and compare. Which word actually belongs? What phrase surrounds it? Can you now reconstruct that phrase accurately?

Avoid repeating a wrong answer simply because it feels familiar. Use feedback to correct the attempt. Then give yourself another opportunity to produce the corrected wording.

Reading remains useful here. Checking the passage is not the enemy of recall practice; it is how you establish what you should be recalling. The rhythm we encourage is attempt, check, correct, and return.

## A short practice routine

1. Read the passage attentively while Study is available.
2. Begin the exercise and allow yourself a genuine recall attempt.
3. Use permitted help when you need it.
4. Check the correct wording rather than rushing past feedback.
5. Reconstruct the phrase and continue through the passage.
6. Return at a later opportunity and see what you can supply with less help.

These steps describe a way to use the game thoughtfully. They are not a claim that one routine fits every person, and they do not make the game a substitute for reading Scripture in context.

## Beyond getting the next answer right

The deeper aim is to make the words increasingly available when the screen is not supplying them. A correct answer during a supported exercise is encouraging; a later, less supported attempt gives you another useful check.

That is why we ask you to remember before showing you more. We want to give your own recall a place in the process, while keeping correction and appropriate support available.
