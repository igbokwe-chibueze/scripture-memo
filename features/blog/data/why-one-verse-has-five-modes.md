You have just placed the last word in a verse. The next screen presents the same passage as a Puzzle. After that comes Swap, then Cue, then Fill. Why keep returning to wording you have already worked with?

Scripture Memo uses five modes because we want repetition to involve different jobs. The passage remains central, while the screen changes what it supplies and what you must do yourself. Our intention is to make repeated encounters purposeful, rather than ask you to perform the same interaction indefinitely.

## Repetition is more than another look

You can repeat an encounter with a passage by reading it again. You can also repeat it by reconstructing its phrases, checking its order, or supplying missing words. These activities make different demands, even when the text is unchanged.

Dunlosky and colleagues' review of learning techniques rated practice testing and distributed practice highly for their usefulness across learning conditions. Rereading received a lower overall utility assessment. That supports including more than repeated exposure in a learning routine; it does not mean that reading is unimportant. [Read the review of learning techniques.](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/journals/pspi/learning-techniques.html)

Our five-mode sequence is a product design informed by that general direction. The research does not establish that our exact combination or ordering is optimal for every person. We should evaluate the sequence by what it helps people remember, not assume that variety alone guarantees learning.

## Drag & Drop: connect the word to its sentence

Drag & Drop supplies a word bank and receiving gaps. You select a word and place it where it belongs. On a touch device, selecting the word and tapping a gap is also supported.

The bank makes this a supported task. You do not have to produce every answer without seeing possible words. You do, however, need to examine how a word fits into the verse.

Try reading the surrounding phrase before placing a tile. After placement, read the restored wording. That keeps the interaction connected to the passage instead of turning it into a purely visual matching exercise.

## Puzzle: put phrases back together

Puzzle works with groups of words. Instead of placing only isolated words, you reconstruct larger pieces of the passage.

Our intention is to make phrase structure visible. A short group can give you something manageable to work with, but completing the tile placement is not the whole purpose. The phrase belongs within a sentence and a wider passage.

Ask how the group connects to what comes before and after it. A successful placement should leave you attending to the words, not merely noticing that a slot has been filled.

## Swap: notice when familiar words are in the wrong order

Swap asks you to exchange highlighted words to restore their sequence. You can recognize every word on the screen and still have an incorrectly ordered passage.

That makes this a different job from selecting an absent word. You need to inspect the sentence as it currently stands, identify a problem, and repair it.

After swapping, read the result. Does it match the verse you are learning? Avoid treating a plausible-sounding sentence as proof that the wording is correct.

## Cue: use a starting signal to supply a word

Cue provides first-letter support for missing words. The letter gives you a starting point, but you still have to supply the word.

This is our intended bridge between supplied answers and less supported production. If several words could begin with the same letter, consider the surrounding text and the translation being practiced.

Cue is a game mode. The separate Hint System offers additional help where the Journey Stage allows it. They are not two names for the same feature.

## Fill: produce wording with less help

Fill asks you to enter missing words without Cue's first-letter support. The screen gives you less of what you need to produce.

That can feel harder even though the passage is familiar. Notice which words come readily and which phrase needs another careful attempt. Check feedback and correct the wording rather than entering guesses as quickly as possible.

Fill is still a game task with its own context. Recalling the entire passage later without visible text is another useful personal check.

## The challenge changes across the waypoint too

Glimmer, Glow, and Radiance each contain all five modes. The hidden-word proportion increases across those stages, so a later encounter asks you to reconstruct more of the passage.

Glimmer hides roughly 20–35% of the words, Glow roughly 40–60%, and Radiance roughly 70–100%. A full waypoint therefore includes fifteen mode completions. Fifteen is the current structure, not a scientifically established ideal number of repetitions.

The later challenge stages also require a return after a minimum twelve-hour wait. That separates some of the repeated work rather than placing every encounter in one uninterrupted session.

## Make the repeated work attentive

You can approach any mode with one useful question: what is this exercise asking me to notice about the passage?

Sometimes the answer is a missing word. Sometimes it is the relationship between phrases or the order of familiar wording. Sometimes it is what you can produce with less help.

We use several modes to provide those different opportunities and make practice approachable. The learning purpose is still the passage itself. Let the controls lead your attention back to its words and meaning.
