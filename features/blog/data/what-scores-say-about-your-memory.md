Your Beacon Level rises. A badge unlocks. The streak flame grows. These moments make progress visible, and we want them to feel encouraging. But what do they actually tell you about your memory?

They tell you something about completed activity and achievements under the game's rules. They do not tell you exactly how much Scripture you can independently recall today.

Understanding that difference can help you enjoy the rewards without losing sight of the reason for practicing.

## What Beacon XP records

Beacon XP is permanent, non-spendable progression earned through eligible gameplay. It contributes to your Beacon Level. Eligible weekly XP also participates in league competition, with the weekly total operating separately from lifetime progression.

A higher total reflects more eligible rewarded work. It does not function as a direct measurement of memory strength. The game cannot infer every verse you can recite away from the screen by looking at that number.

Replays, Vault review, failed attempts, and administrator testing do not award Beacon XP. That does not make every one of those activities educationally equivalent, and it does not make a useful review worthless just because it adds no new XP.

## Glow Points, badges, and Crowns have different jobs

Glow Points are the game's spendable currency. Badges recognize defined achievements and award Glow Points. Crowns are separate, non-spendable prestige associated with eligible Saint League finishes.

Those systems should be described accurately rather than combined into one vague learning score. Currency, progression, achievements, and competition are different parts of the game.

None certifies spiritual maturity or provides a complete evaluation of learning. We would not want a player to conclude that someone with fewer points has engaged less thoughtfully with Scripture.

## What a completed challenge proves

Completing a mode means that the attempt met its defined requirements. Completing Glimmer, Glow, or Radiance means that all five modes in that challenge stage were finished.

Those are real milestones within the game. They also occur under particular conditions: supplied text, banks of words, cues, recent practice, and the current Journey Stage's rules.

It remains useful to ask a later question: what can you recall with less of that support? A completion record and an independent recall attempt give you different information.

Learning research distinguishes practice performance from longer-term learning. Soderstrom and Bjork's review explains why immediate performance is not a complete guide to what remains available later. [Read the review of learning and performance.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25910388/)

That distinction informs our intention to look beyond rewarded activity when thinking about the learning outcome.

## Why we include visible rewards at all

A journey can be difficult to follow if completed effort leaves no clear trace. We use rewards, progress indicators, and celebrations to acknowledge work and make the next step legible.

You can see that a challenge stage is complete, that a waypoint has advanced, or that an eligible achievement has been recorded. Meemo and the visual world are intended to make those moments welcoming.

These are our design intentions. We do not claim that our specific reward system has been shown to improve Scripture retention. A reward can make participation appealing without proving that the material has been remembered more durably.

We should evaluate whether the systems serve the central learning purpose, rather than judge them only by how often people return to collect something.

## Use two kinds of questions

When looking at your progress, you can ask an activity question: what work have I completed? The game provides useful records for that.

You can also ask a recall question: what wording can I bring to mind without looking? An occasional personal check can help you explore that, even when the app does not record it.

Choose a practiced passage, begin from its reference, and attempt to say or write it. Compare the result with the selected translation. Notice what was accurate and which phrase deserves review.

That is a suggested personal routine, not a new graded app feature or a scientifically validated personal memory score. Its purpose is to connect the visible journey to what you want available beyond the interface.

## Practice can matter without a new reward

A mastered verse in the Vault can still deserve attention. An older phrase may become uncertain. You may want to reconnect familiar wording with its wider context.

Returning for that reason is worthwhile even when the attempt does not raise your Beacon Level. The passage is not valuable only when it is attached to a new reward.

Likewise, a mistake in review does not erase earlier work. It identifies something useful to practice now. Progress should leave room for returning and correcting, rather than make old passages feel untouchable after a milestone.

## Let the celebration point back to the purpose

Enjoy the achievement. You completed something the game asked you to do, and it is reasonable to acknowledge the effort.

Then keep the wider goal in view: words you can recall, consider in context, and use thoughtfully when the screen is closed. A visible reward should encourage that journey, not replace it as the definition of success.
