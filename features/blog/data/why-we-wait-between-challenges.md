You finish Glimmer, feel encouraged, and want to continue. Then the game tells you that Glow will become available after twelve hours. Why interrupt a good session?

The waiting period is intended to create a return to the passage after time has passed. Scripture Memo is designed around more than completing everything in one uninterrupted sitting. We want the journey to include opportunities to discover what you can remember later.

This is where spaced practice enters the design.

## What spaced practice means

Spaced practice means distributing encounters with material across separated learning opportunities. Massed practice concentrates those encounters closely together. Repeating a verse ten times in one sitting and returning to it across several sessions are therefore different practice schedules.

Cepeda and colleagues' review and quantitative synthesis found evidence for the benefits of distributed practice in verbal-memory tasks. It also examined how spacing interacts with the interval before a later memory test. [Read the review of distributed practice.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)

For Scripture Memo, our design interpretation is that the passage should return after the first encounter is no longer immediately fresh. The question becomes more than “Can I finish this while I have just been working on it?” It includes “What can I recover when I come back?”

## Our current rhythm

Each waypoint contains three challenge stages, in order:

- **Glimmer:** available when the waypoint unlocks, with roughly 20–35% of the words hidden.
- **Glow:** available twelve elapsed hours after Glimmer is completed, with roughly 40–60% hidden.
- **Radiance:** available twelve elapsed hours after Glow is completed, with roughly 70–100% hidden.

Each stage contains the five modes: Drag & Drop, Puzzle, Swap, Cue, and Fill. You return to the same passage with a larger portion of its wording hidden.

The wait is measured from completion. It is not a midnight reset, and you do not need to finish the journey on three consecutive calendar dates. The current name is the Three-Stage Challenge because the sequence consists of stages rather than a promise about calendar days.

## Why twelve hours rather than a different interval?

Twelve hours is our current design choice. It creates predictable separation without requiring a full twenty-four-hour wait between stages. It is not a scientifically proven best interval for every learner or passage.

Research on optimizing distributed practice has examined different gaps and later retention intervals. It gives us reason to consider timing carefully rather than assume one gap is universally optimal. [Read Cepeda and colleagues' research on practice timing.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19439395/)

Our current system does not estimate your personal forgetting curve or calculate the ideal next review time for each verse. It supplies a structured minimum delay. We should describe that honestly rather than imply that a personalized scheduler is already operating behind the countdown.

## An unlock time is not a deadline

Imagine finishing Glimmer at eight in the morning. Glow becomes available at eight that evening. You may choose to return then, but you can also return the next morning or another suitable time.

The countdown tells you when you may continue. It does not mean that a later return has missed the learning opportunity. Nor does it mean that you should interrupt sleep, work, family responsibilities, or worship to start at the earliest minute.

Choose a rhythm you can sustain. A practice plan that fits your life is easier to return to than one that makes every countdown feel urgent.

## What to do during the wait

You do not have to keep the app open. Continue with your day. When you return, the game will determine whether the next challenge is ready.

Study access follows its own progression rules: it is available before the challenge begins, closes during the challenge period, and reopens permanently after Radiance. That is a rule about the game's study window, not an instruction to avoid your Bible outside the app.

Reading Scripture, reflecting, or seeking context beyond the game remains part of your own practice. We do not want a cooldown to be mistaken for a spiritual restriction.

Where review opportunities are available elsewhere in your journey, those can also give you something useful to do. But waiting does not need to be filled with more screen time merely to make it feel productive.

## There is a second kind of return

The three challenge stages provide an initial pattern of separated practice. The wider curriculum can bring the same verse back at later Learn, Recall, Strengthen, and Master waypoints.

Those later appearances operate at another scale. They return a passage after you have progressed through other material. Their elapsed timing depends partly on how quickly you move through the curriculum, so a waypoint position is not the same as a fixed number of days for every player.

Together, the two structures express our intention to make returning part of the journey. Neither means that completing a countdown guarantees durable memory.

## What we want you to notice when you return

At the start of Glow, see what you can supply before asking for extra help. Which phrases remain available? Which transition needs another careful attempt? Use feedback to reconnect the wording.

You may remember more than expected or less than hoped. Either way, the return gives you a chance to practice the passage again under a different condition from the first session.

The wait exists to support that return. We want Scripture Memo to encourage a rhythm of learning, leaving, remembering, correcting, and coming back—not simply a longer sitting at the same screen.
