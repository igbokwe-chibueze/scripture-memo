You check your verse and discover that something is wrong. Perhaps two words are exchanged, a phrase is incomplete, or you have supplied wording from another translation. It is tempting to rush straight into another attempt or decide that memorization is not for you.

There is a more useful next step: examine what the answer is telling you.

An error does not need to become a verdict about your ability. It can identify a particular piece of wording that deserves attention. What happens after the error matters.

## The correction is part of the learning opportunity

Janet Metcalfe's review of learning from errors discusses evidence that errorful learning followed by corrective feedback can benefit learning. The correction is essential to that description: an error that is noticed and repaired is not the same situation as an error that remains unexamined. [Read the review of learning from errors.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27648988/)

This supports our intention to make feedback useful. It does not mean that every mistake is beneficial, that you should deliberately choose wrong answers, or that frustration itself is an effective teaching method.

Our goal is accurate recall. Errors are occasions to work toward it, not a separate achievement to collect.

## First, identify what kind of problem occurred

Different wrong answers can call for different responses.

You may have remembered the right words in the wrong order. You may have omitted a short linking word. You may have entered a sensible synonym instead of the wording in the selected translation. Or a touch interaction may have placed something somewhere you did not intend.

These are not all the same problem. A word-order issue asks for attention to sequence. A translation mix-up asks you to clarify which text you are learning. A control problem asks us to improve the interface.

Before trying again, take a moment to understand the difference between your attempt and the expected wording.

## Work with a phrase, not only an isolated error marker

Suppose one word is incorrect. Read the phrase around it. What comes before the word? What follows it? How does the whole phrase fit into the sentence?

Our practical suggestion is to reconnect the correction to its context. If you only memorize that one tile should be moved to a different slot, you may miss the wording you are trying to remember.

The same principle applies to Swap. After exchanging two highlighted words, read the resulting sentence. Check whether you have restored the passage rather than merely made it sound plausible.

In a typed mode, notice exactly what differed. Avoid repeatedly submitting a familiar guess without checking whether it matches the selected text.

## Give the corrected wording another chance

After examining the right phrase, try to reconstruct it again. Where the exercise allows, make another attempt before immediately requesting more help.

You are checking whether the correction has become something you can supply, not only something you recognize when it is shown.

If you still need permitted assistance, use it. The purpose is to move toward a successful next attempt. It is not to prove that you can remain stuck for a long time without help.

Later, a returning challenge gives you another opportunity to find out what remains available. An immediate correct retry and a later recall attempt answer different practical questions.

## A small example of using feedback well

Imagine that the same transition between two phrases causes trouble in several attempts. You know the beginning of the verse and the ending, but the connection is uncertain.

Instead of racing through the whole passage again, attend to that transition. Read the surrounding wording when it is available, identify the linking words, and reconstruct the short stretch carefully.

Then place it back into the whole passage. The next task is to remember the sentence, not merely remember where the error appeared on yesterday's board.

This is a suggested way to use feedback, not a personalized diagnosis from the app. Scripture Memo does not currently infer every cause of a mistake or prescribe an individually optimized repair routine.

## Keep challenge separate from confusion

An exercise can reasonably ask you to recall a missing word. It should not ask you to guess how its controls work.

If a selected word cannot be placed where you expect, the receiving gaps are unclear, or instructions do not explain the task, that is product feedback we need. Interface confusion should not be defended as useful learning difficulty.

Likewise, different input devices and typing familiarity can affect an attempt. A wrong submission does not tell us everything about what a person understands or remembers.

## Return without turning the mistake into your identity

A particular attempt can be incorrect while the larger learning journey continues. Earlier completed work remains part of that journey. The useful question is what to do next.

Check the wording. Notice the difference. Reconstruct the phrase. Return to it later when you can. That sequence gives an error a practical role without making it the center of your experience.

We want feedback to help you continue with greater clarity. The aim is not to avoid every imperfect attempt, but to avoid leaving an imperfect attempt unexamined when it could guide your next step.
