Scripture Memo begins with a familiar experience: you read a passage, feel that it matters, and want to carry it with you. Later, when you try to recall it, the words are incomplete. You remember the idea but lose the sequence. You recognize the verse on a page but cannot bring it to mind without looking.

That gap between encountering Scripture and being able to remember it is the problem we are building Scripture Memo to address.

Our ambition is to help you develop a lasting, thoughtful relationship with the verses you learn. We want practice to be approachable enough to begin, structured enough to guide you, and meaningful enough to return to. The game is the setting for that work. Scripture is the reason for it.

This article explains what we are trying to achieve, how the learning journey works, why we repeat verses, what spacing contributes, and where the science ends and our design decisions begin. It also explains what we are still learning as we build.

## 1. Our philosophy: remember the words, remain attentive to their meaning

Memorizing Scripture can serve many personal purposes: prayer, reflection, encouragement, conversation, teaching, and having familiar words available during an unfamiliar moment. You may arrive with one of those purposes, or simply with a desire to begin reading more attentively.

We want to make that intention easier to act on. A person should not need to invent a curriculum, build a review calendar, and design their own exercises before practicing a first verse. Scripture Memo provides a path and a set of repeatable actions so that more of your attention can go toward the passage itself.

Accurate wording matters in a memorization game. Meaning matters beyond the game. Being able to reproduce a sentence does not automatically mean that you understand its context, interpret it responsibly, or apply it wisely. We therefore see memorization as one part of a larger practice of reading, study, reflection, and lived faith.

The app should support that larger practice rather than present a score as its substitute. A Beacon Level cannot measure spiritual maturity. A streak cannot measure devotion. A completed challenge cannot certify that someone has understood everything a passage teaches.

Our practical goal is more specific: help you become increasingly able to recall the Scripture you have practiced, with less assistance, after time has passed. Our broader hope is that those remembered words become available for thoughtful use beyond the screen.

### A welcoming beginning and an honest challenge

We want a beginner to encounter a manageable first step. Facing a completely blank verse immediately can be discouraging; seeing every answer forever would leave a different problem. The journey needs both support and a way to move beyond it.

That is why the game gradually removes help. Early exercises give you words, phrases, and context to work with. Later exercises ask you to supply more yourself. Over the wider curriculum, familiar verses return under more demanding conditions.

Meemo, our visual world, and the celebrations are intended to make this work feel inviting. They are companions to the learning task. The task itself remains your encounter with the verse: noticing it, reconstructing it, recalling it, correcting it, and returning to it.

## 2. Why familiarity can feel stronger than it is

Consider two experiences. In the first, you read a verse while every word is visible and think, “I know this.” In the second, you cover the text and try to say it from the reference alone. The second experience can reveal gaps that the first concealed.

This is a useful distinction for our design. Recognition asks whether something seems familiar when it is present. Recall asks you to produce information that is not fully available in front of you. Both can belong in practice, but they do not ask the same thing of you.

Research also distinguishes performance during practice from learning that persists later. Soderstrom and Bjork's review describes why immediate ease or success is not a complete measure of durable learning. A practice session can feel smooth without telling us how well someone will remember after a delay. [Read the review on learning and performance.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25910388/)

For Scripture Memo, the implication is a design question: can we help you move from recognizing a passage to recalling it when fewer of its words are visible? That question shapes our hidden-word challenges and the progression from arranging supplied words toward entering missing ones.

It also shapes how we want you to interpret difficulty. Needing to pause is not proof that you are failing. It may simply show that the screen is now asking you to do something more demanding than recognize familiar text.

## 3. The science we draw on—and the claims we will not make

Scripture Memo draws on research about learning and memory. The most relevant ideas include distributed practice, retrieval practice, repeated relearning, corrective feedback, and the difference between immediate performance and later retention.

We need to make an important boundary explicit: research supporting these ideas does not automatically validate every feature in our app. A study of vocabulary learning is not a clinical trial of Scripture Memo. A review of spacing does not prove that our exact twelve-hour cooldown, five-mode sequence, artwork, or reward system produces a particular improvement in biblical memorization.

We are applying established principles to a specific product. The precise combination is our design, and its effectiveness should be examined rather than assumed. Where we describe an intention, we mean an intention. Where we describe an implemented rule, we mean the current game. Where we describe evidence, we link to research so that you can examine it yourself.

We do not promise perfect memory, a fixed percentage improvement, or permanent protection from forgetting. We do not claim to diagnose learning difficulties or provide a treatment. We want to build a useful learning environment and be honest about what would count as evidence that it helps.

## 4. Spaced practice: why returning matters

### What spacing means

Spaced practice means revisiting material across separated learning opportunities. Its contrast is massed practice: concentrating those opportunities into one closely packed session. “Spaced repetition” describes the repeated encounters; “retention” describes remembering later. Spacing is an approach to practice, while retention is an outcome we hope that practice supports.

Cepeda and colleagues' quantitative review found substantial evidence for distributed practice in verbal-memory tasks. Their analysis also showed that the relationship between the gap between sessions and the later retention interval matters. There is more to effective spacing than choosing one universal delay. [Read the review of distributed practice.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)

For a learner, the practical idea is simple: completing a verse once is a beginning, and returning to it later is part of the work. A later encounter asks what remains available after the first session is no longer immediately fresh.

### How spacing appears in Scripture Memo

We use two different structures to create returns to a passage.

First, each waypoint contains Glimmer, Glow, and Radiance. Glow becomes available twelve elapsed hours after Glimmer is completed. Radiance becomes available twelve elapsed hours after Glow is completed. These are minimum waiting periods between challenge stages.

Second, a verse can return at later waypoints in the wider journey. It can appear in Learn, then Recall, then Strengthen, then Master. These later appearances create another opportunity to revisit the same passage after progressing elsewhere.

The first structure provides a predictable initial rhythm. The second places review within the ongoing curriculum. They work at different scales and should not be confused with each other.

### Why twelve hours is a design choice

Twelve hours is our current product rule. It creates separation between the three challenge stages without requiring that each stage occupy a different calendar day. It is not a scientifically established best interval for every verse, learner, or retention goal.

Research on distributed practice has investigated different gaps and delayed tests, including delays extending over months. That work supports taking the relationship between practice timing and the intended retention horizon seriously. It does not establish a single ideal interval for all circumstances. [Read Cepeda and colleagues' work on optimizing distributed practice.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19439395/)

Our current curriculum is structured rather than individually adaptive. We do not presently calculate a personalized forgetting curve and schedule each verse at a predicted optimal moment. We also do not know how much you recall outside the app merely because a cooldown has ended.

That distinction matters. Scripture Memo currently provides spaced opportunities and progressive review. A fully individualized spaced-repetition scheduler would be a further capability, not a feature we should imply already exists.

## 5. Retrieval practice: remembering is something you do

Retrieval practice means trying to bring information back from memory. Reading a verse provides an encounter with the text. Trying to supply a hidden word adds a different activity: attempting to recover something that is no longer fully visible.

In a study of foreign-language vocabulary, Karpicke and Roediger found that repeated retrieval after initial learning supported later recall, and that additional study did not produce the same benefit under the conditions they examined. This is one reason learning researchers distinguish retrieval from simply seeing an answer again. [Read the study on retrieval and learning.](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408)

Our design interpretation is that memorization practice should include moments when you must produce an answer. We therefore move beyond asking you to read a verse or tap a familiar-looking option. Some modes supply much of the material; others require you to enter missing words with less support.

We do not treat every tap or drag as equally strong retrieval practice. Moving a visible tile can involve recognizing where it belongs, while typing a missing word can demand more independent recall. The value of the sequence is that it gives you several ways to engage before asking you to do more unaided.

### Give recall a chance before asking for help

A useful way to play is to make a genuine attempt before opening a hint. Pause, consider the surrounding phrase, and see whether the word comes to mind. If it does not, use the available support, examine the correct wording, and try again.

The purpose is not to make you struggle indefinitely. It is to leave room for an attempt at remembering before the answer is supplied. Help should move you toward a successful next attempt rather than become a reason to stop attempting recall altogether.

## 6. Repetition: the same verse, a different job each time

Repetition is central to Scripture Memo, but repetition alone is not a complete explanation of our approach. Reading the same sentence repeatedly, rebuilding its phrases, and recalling its missing words are different activities.

Dunlosky and colleagues' review of ten learning techniques rated practice testing and distributed practice highly for their broad usefulness across learning conditions. Rereading received a lower overall utility assessment. That does not mean reading has no value; it means repeated exposure should not be our only strategy for learning that needs to last. [Read the review of effective learning techniques.](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/journals/pspi/learning-techniques.html)

In our game, the repeated verse stays constant while the job changes. You may first recognize missing words, then reconstruct phrases, then correct their order, then use an initial-letter cue, then supply missing text. Across challenge stages, more of the passage is hidden. Across Journey Stages, support and timing conditions change.

These are our chosen ways of making a return to the passage purposeful. We want repeated practice to help you notice wording, relationships, sequence, and the places where your memory still needs support.

A completed waypoint contains fifteen mode completions: five modes in each of three challenge stages. That is the current structure, not a claim that fifteen is a scientifically optimal number of repetitions. Some learners will need more practice; some exercises will be easier than others; and longer-term review remains necessary.

## 7. Your first layer of progress: Glimmer, Glow, and Radiance

Every waypoint gives you one assigned passage and a Three-Stage Challenge. Here is what each challenge stage asks of you.

### Glimmer: begin with support

Glimmer is available when the waypoint unlocks. It hides approximately 20–35% of the verse's words. Much of the text remains visible, giving you a supported first encounter with the exercises.

The aim is to let you work with the passage before asking for extensive independent recall. Use that visible context attentively: notice how the sentence develops and where the missing words belong. The remaining text is part of the learning opportunity.

### Glow: return and recall more

Glow unlocks twelve elapsed hours after you complete Glimmer. It hides approximately 40–60% of the words. You return to the same passage with less of its wording supplied.

This return gives you a chance to discover what you can recover after a delay. Some words may come easily; others may surprise you. Treat those differences as useful information about what to practice.

### Radiance: work with much less visible text

Radiance unlocks twelve elapsed hours after Glow is completed. It hides approximately 70–100% of the words. The challenge therefore asks for substantially more reconstruction or recall, depending on the mode.

Finishing Radiance completes the waypoint and allows the journey to continue to the next available published waypoint. It does not mean that the verse can never be forgotten. It means you have completed this defined set of learning tasks.

### These are stages, not three compulsory calendar days

The names describe the sequence. The waiting periods are measured from completion, not from midnight. If you finish Glimmer at 8 a.m., Glow becomes available at 8 p.m.; it is also fine to return the following morning.

There is no educational reason to disrupt your sleep or other responsibilities just to begin a stage at its earliest available minute. A countdown tells you when a challenge can start. It does not tell you when you must start.

## 8. The five modes and the purpose of each

Each challenge stage uses the same order: Drag & Drop, Puzzle, Swap, Cue, and Fill. A consistent order helps you know what comes next while the passage and level of support change.

### Drag & Drop: attend to the missing word

Drag & Drop presents gaps and a bank of available words. You choose a word and place it where it belongs. On touch devices, you can also select a word and tap a receiving gap.

Our intention is to focus attention on the fit between a word and its sentence. The bank provides support, but placing the word still asks you to examine the passage. Read the surrounding words rather than treating the screen only as a collection of shapes to match.

### Puzzle: rebuild phrases

Puzzle asks you to work with groups of words. Instead of dealing only with isolated words, you reconstruct larger pieces of the passage.

This is our way of giving phrase structure a visible role. A phrase can be a manageable unit to practice, but the goal is still to restore the whole passage. We do not claim that a particular tile length is an established optimal memory unit.

As you place a group, consider how it connects to the words before and after it. The exercise should lead your attention back to the sentence, not stop at completing the tile placement.

### Swap: repair the sequence

Swap presents movable words that need to be returned to their proper order. You select two highlighted words to exchange their positions.

The task asks a different question from filling a blank: can you detect where the sequence is wrong? A sentence can contain familiar words and still have the wrong order. We use this mode to make that distinction explicit.

Try to read the resulting sentence after each change. Avoid relying entirely on the appearance of the tiles. The passage is the thing you are reconstructing.

### Cue: recall with a starting signal

Cue uses first-letter support for the missing words. That starting signal offers more help than an unsupported blank while still requiring you to supply the word.

Our intention is to provide a bridge between fully supplied answers and less supported production. If the initial letter immediately suggests a word, check it against the surrounding verse. A plausible word is not necessarily the wording of the passage you are learning.

Cue is the fourth game mode. The separate Hint System provides additional help where the Journey Stage allows it; it is not another name for this mode.

### Fill: produce missing wording with less support

Fill asks you to enter the missing words without the first-letter scaffolding used in Cue. It gives you a more direct opportunity to discover which wording you can supply yourself.

Completing Fill is a meaningful step in the current challenge. Nevertheless, the surrounding text and the recent practice session may still help you. Reciting a whole passage later, away from the game, remains a different and useful check.

### Why several modes rather than one?

We chose several modes to give the practice sequence different demands and make repeated work more approachable. The variety is a design decision, not evidence that each learner belongs to a fixed “learning style,” or that five modes are inherently better than every alternative.

The sequence is something we should continue evaluating. A successful design would help people retain and independently recall Scripture, rather than merely become skilled at the controls.

## 9. Your second layer of progress: Learn, Recall, Strengthen, and Master

The Three-Stage Challenge happens within a waypoint. Journey Stages describe a verse's later appearances across the curriculum. The same passage can therefore return at different waypoints under different Journey Stage conditions.

### Learn

Learn is the supported introduction. Hints are available, and there is no time limit per mode attempt. You have room to become familiar with the verse and the exercises.

### Recall

Recall revisits the verse later in the journey. Hints remain available, and each mode attempt has a five-minute limit. The passage is familiar, but you are being asked to return to it after moving further through the curriculum.

### Strengthen

Strengthen removes hints and gives each mode attempt a three-minute limit. Our intention is to ask for more independent performance under a more demanding condition.

### Master

Master removes hints and gives each mode attempt a two-minute limit. It is a defined milestone within the game. Further Master appearances may be added to the curriculum, and review remains worthwhile after the milestone has been earned.

At each Journey Stage waypoint, you still complete Glimmer, Glow, and Radiance. A later Recall waypoint is not simply one quick question about an older verse; it includes the same three-stage structure.

### What the timers mean—and what they do not

These time limits are product rules, not claims that speed measures faith, intelligence, or the full quality of learning. Device input, typing familiarity, and individual circumstances can affect performance.

The timer applies to one mode attempt. An expired attempt can be retried without undoing earlier completed modes. Leaving the screen or refreshing does not pause or extend the attempt. Learn remains untimed.

We should evaluate whether timed stages serve the intended learning experience. Making a task harder is not automatically an educational improvement. A challenge that prevents a learner from meaningfully practicing needs a different design response from a challenge that encourages useful effort.

## 10. Returning after success: why “I got it right” is not the end

Getting a verse right once is worth celebrating. It is also worth returning after that success.

Research on successive relearning examines learning material to a criterion and then returning to relearn it in later sessions. Dunlosky and Rawson studied repeated relearning at weekly intervals and found that later relearning changed the importance of the initial learning criterion. The broader lesson for our design is to examine what repeated returns contribute, rather than treating initial success as the whole story. [Read the study of successive relearning.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27027887/)

Our curriculum's repeated Journey Stage appearances are inspired by that general direction. They are not a reproduction of this study's exact procedure or schedule.

The timing of later waypoints also depends on how quickly you move through the game. A fixed position in a curriculum does not create the same elapsed review interval for every player. We need to remain honest about that limitation when describing our approach to spacing.

The Vault provides another place to revisit eligible verses, including those marked as mastered. Use it as an opportunity to continue practicing, rather than a display case for passages that should never need attention again.

## 11. Errors, feedback, and useful effort

An incorrect answer tells you something about your current attempt. Perhaps two words have been exchanged, a phrase is incomplete, or a similar expression has replaced the one in the selected translation.

Metcalfe's review of learning from errors describes evidence that making errors followed by corrective feedback can support learning. The correction matters: generating an error and leaving it unexamined is not the same learning situation as noticing and repairing it. [Read the review of learning from errors.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27648988/)

In Scripture Memo, feedback and retry opportunities are meant to support that repair process. When something is wrong, slow down enough to examine the wording. Reconstruct the relevant phrase, then try to produce it again.

Our intended balance is effort with support. We do not want every exercise to be effortless, and we do not want frustration to become the activity. A useful next step might be another attempt, an available hint, or returning to the passage when Study access permits it.

If a particular interface interaction gets in your way, that is feedback about our product. Confusing controls and unclear instructions should not be defended as helpful learning difficulty.

## 12. Study, context, and understanding

Before recall practice, make time to read the passage attentively. Notice who is speaking, what the surrounding discussion concerns, and how the verse connects to nearby verses. Use the available study material as a starting point for understanding rather than a replacement for reading Scripture in context.

Study access follows the game's progression rules. It is available before a waypoint's challenge begins, closes during the challenge period, and reopens permanently after Radiance. This separates the study window from the period in which the game is asking you to practice recall.

That restriction belongs to the game experience. It is not a statement that you should avoid your Bible outside the app. If you need to read, reflect, seek guidance, or revisit context, your wider practice matters more than treating an interface rule as a spiritual obligation.

You can also ask yourself questions that no tile can answer for you: What is this passage saying? What might I misunderstand if I isolate this line? How would I explain it in my own words? Where would further study help?

Those questions are suggestions for thoughtful practice, not additional automatically graded features. The current game checks defined answers; it does not evaluate the depth of your interpretation or personal reflection.

## 13. Rest, sleep, and a sustainable rhythm

Learning should fit within a life that includes rest. Rasch and Born's review describes evidence for sleep's role in memory consolidation, including the contribution of sleep processes to stabilizing and reorganizing memories. It does not establish that our twelve-hour wait guarantees a sleep-related benefit. [Read the review of sleep and memory.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23589831/)

Our practical recommendation is to choose a rhythm you can sustain. A newly available challenge can wait until a suitable time. If the earliest unlock falls late at night, you can return after resting.

We do not require a perfect schedule. What matters to the learning goal is returning to meaningful practice over time, including after an interruption. The game should make the next step easier to find when you come back.

## 14. What the rewards are for

Learning progress can be hard to see from one session to the next. We use visible milestones to acknowledge completed work and make the journey legible.

Glow Points are the game's spendable currency. Beacon XP is permanent, non-spendable progression that contributes to Beacon Levels; eligible weekly XP also participates in league competition. Crowns are separate, non-spendable league prestige. Badges recognize defined achievements and award Glow Points.

These systems describe activity and achievements under the game's rules. They are not direct measures of memory strength. A player with more XP has completed more eligible rewarded work; that number does not tell us exactly how many verses they can recall without support today.

Replays and Vault practice can still be worthwhile even when they do not award Beacon XP. We want you to return to a passage because it is worth remembering, including when there is no new progression reward attached.

Streaks are intended to make consistency visible. If a streak ends, your earlier effort has not been erased. Return to the next useful practice opportunity rather than treating a broken number as a reason to abandon the journey.

Competition may motivate some people and distract others. Our commitment is that the central purpose remains learning Scripture. We should judge reward systems by how they support that purpose, not only by whether they increase time spent in the app.

## 15. A practical example of the journey

Imagine that you are beginning a passage in a Learn waypoint.

First, read it in the selected translation while Study is available. Take time with its surrounding context. Notice its phrases and how they connect. You are preparing to remember a passage, not simply preparing to clear a screen.

Next, begin Glimmer. Work through Drag & Drop, Puzzle, Swap, Cue, and Fill. Where a word is missing, give yourself a genuine chance to remember it. Where you make an error, inspect the correction rather than rushing past it.

When Glimmer is complete, continue with your day. Glow becomes available after twelve hours. Return when it suits your schedule and work through the five modes with more of the wording hidden.

After Glow is complete and another twelve hours have elapsed, Radiance becomes available. Complete its five modes, then celebrate finishing the waypoint. Study access reopens, and the next available waypoint becomes part of your journey.

Later, the same passage may return in Recall, Strengthen, and Master waypoints. Those returns give you further opportunities to practice after intervening learning. You can also use the review opportunities the game provides.

Finally, try an occasional check away from the controls: begin from the reference, say or write the passage without looking, then compare it with your Bible. This is a suggested personal exercise, not an app-recorded test. It can help you distinguish remembering the verse from remembering how to complete its game board.

## 16. How to make the most of your practice

- **Read before rushing.** Start with the passage and its context while Study is available.
- **Attempt recall before revealing help.** Allow an honest attempt, then use available support when it is needed.
- **Examine the correction.** Notice exactly which word or phrase differed, and reconstruct the correct wording.
- **Read the restored sentence.** After a placement or swap, reconnect the tile operation to the whole passage.
- **Return after success.** Completing a challenge is a milestone; later review is part of remembering.
- **Keep one translation clear in your mind.** Know which wording the current session is asking you to reproduce. Similar wording from another translation can otherwise be confusing.
- **Make room for rest.** An unlock time is permission to begin, not an appointment that overrides your life.
- **Practice beyond the interface occasionally.** Try recalling a verse without the bank, tiles, or visible context, then check it accurately.
- **Keep meaning in view.** Read, reflect, and seek context rather than assuming accurate recitation settles every question of interpretation.

These suggestions are meant to support your use of the game. They are not a promise that one routine fits every learner or a substitute for individual educational advice where it is needed.

## 17. How we are building and improving Scripture Memo

We are developing the game in stages. That means building the learning and progression rules, making the exercises usable, improving the visual experience, and checking that the underlying behavior remains reliable as the interface changes.

We want each layer to serve a clear purpose. The map should show where you are going. Waypoint details should show what is ready, what is complete, and what requires a wait. A mode introduction should explain the task before an attempt begins. The active board should make selecting, placing, typing, checking, and retrying understandable. A completion screen should accurately acknowledge the work and rewards that were earned.

This work includes practical details that are easy to overlook: touch-friendly controls, receiving gaps that respond when a word is selected, readable light and dark themes, clear help, audio controls, and reduced-motion preferences. These details are about removing avoidable friction so that the passage receives your attention.

We also protect the integrity of progress. Cooldowns, attempts, answer checks, and rewards are enforced by the server. A display animation or a changed device clock does not decide whether a challenge has been completed. Reliable progress is part of making the game trustworthy.

Our journal has a role in that process too. We want the reasoning behind the game to be available to players, not hidden in private development conversations. Guides should explain the current rules, and articles like this one should explain the intentions behind them. When the product changes, those explanations should change with it.

### What we should measure as the product develops

The most important questions concern what people can remember after practice, not only what they can complete during it. Useful future evaluation could examine delayed recall, how much support is needed, whether errors recur, and whether players can recall passages away from the interface.

We should also examine whether people understand the instructions, whether particular controls create avoidable errors, and whether the experience remains approachable after the first few sessions.

These are evaluation goals, not a claim that a research trial or comprehensive learning-outcomes dashboard is already in place. Any future research or additional data collection needs an appropriate, transparent process. We should not infer durable retention from engagement statistics alone.

## 18. What exists now, and what remains an ambition

The current design includes sequential waypoints, the three challenge stages, the five ordered modes, Journey Stage conditions, study windows, hints where allowed, later curriculum appearances, review opportunities, and visible progression rewards.

Our ambition is to improve how well that combination supports lasting recall and thoughtful engagement. Features such as individually adaptive review timing, scientifically validated optimal intervals, or automatic detection of long-term mastery should not be assumed from the language of spacing and repetition. They are not established capabilities of the current system.

We want to keep that distinction visible. It is better to explain a modest implemented feature clearly than to make a large educational promise that the product has not demonstrated.

## 19. What “Master” should mean to a player

Within Scripture Memo, Master is a defined Journey Stage and a milestone in progression. In human memory, learning does not become immune to time because an interface marks it complete.

You may return to a mastered verse and discover that a phrase needs attention. That does not make the earlier achievement meaningless. It gives you a useful next practice task.

Our hope is that the game helps you develop confidence grounded in repeated experience: you have encountered a verse, worked with its wording, recalled it with less help, and returned to it later. That is a more useful kind of confidence than assuming a completion badge is a guarantee of permanent recall.

## 20. The invitation

Scripture Memo is being built for the moment when a good intention needs a practical next step. Read a passage. Work with it carefully. Try to recall it. Notice what needs correction. Return after time has passed. Continue, with support that gradually gives you more responsibility for remembering.

The path is structured, but your purpose for walking it is personal. You may be learning one verse that matters deeply to you or beginning a much longer journey. We want the game to make that practice clearer and more approachable while keeping Scripture at its center.

Our promise is to keep building thoughtfully, explain our decisions honestly, and remain open to evidence about what helps. Our hope is that what begins as a challenge on a screen becomes a passage you can remember, consider, and carry into the rest of your life.
